1. What is phishing?

Phishing is a cyber attack where criminals pretend to be a trusted person or organization to trick you into revealing sensitive information, clicking a malicious link, or opening a harmful attachment.

🔑

Credential theft

Stolen passwords for email, banking, and work accounts.

💸

Financial fraud

Fake invoices, payment redirection, and card theft.

🦠

Malware & ransomware

Malicious attachments or links that install harmful software.

🏢

Network breaches

A single click can give attackers a way into a company network.

Why it works: phishing targets people, not technology. Most breaches begin with a human decision, which is why awareness is one of the strongest defenses.

2. Types of phishing attacks

📧

Email phishing

Mass emails pretending to be banks, delivery companies, or online services.

🎯

Spear phishing

Personalized messages aimed at one person, using details like your name, job, or colleagues.

🐋

Whaling

Targets executives and decision makers with high-value requests.

💬

Smishing

Phishing by SMS: "Your parcel is held, pay a small fee here."

📞

Vishing

Voice calls from fake "bank security" or "tech support" agents.

🌐

Fake websites

Look-alike login pages designed to capture what you type.

📱

Quishing

Malicious QR codes that lead to fake pages.

👔

BEC

Business Email Compromise: someone poses as a boss or vendor to request payments.

4. How to recognize fake websites

Fake sites copy the look of a real service. The reliable giveaway is the domain name: the part right before the first single /. These are fictional examples.

https://login.examplebank.com/signinLEGIT STYLEThe real domain is examplebank.com. "login" is just a subdomain of it.
https://examplebank.com.account-verify.example/signinFAKEThe real domain is account-verify.example. "examplebank.com" is only a decoy subdomain.
https://examp1ebank.com/signinFAKETyposquatting: the letter "l" is replaced with the number "1".
https://examplebank-secure-login.comFAKEExtra words like "secure", "verify", or "support" glued to a brand name are a classic trick.
http://192.0.2.45/examplebank/loginFAKEA raw IP address instead of a proper domain name.
🔒

HTTPS is not proof

The padlock only means the connection is encrypted. Criminals can get HTTPS certificates too.

⌨️

Type it yourself

Open a new tab and type the address, or use your bookmark, instead of following a link.

🖼️

Look for oddities

Blurry logos, broken layout, odd pop-ups, and pages asking for far more data than usual.

🔍

Check the details

Missing contact info, no privacy policy, or a very new domain are warning signs.

5. Social engineering tactics

Social engineering manipulates human emotions. Attackers rely on a handful of psychological triggers.

⏰

Urgency

"Act now or lose access." Time pressure blocks careful thinking.

👑

Authority

Pretending to be a CEO, police officer, bank, or IT department.

😨

Fear

Threats of fines, account closure, or legal action.

🎁

Greed

Prizes, refunds, and "exclusive" offers that seem too good to be true.

🤝

Trust & helpfulness

"Can you help me quickly?" from a spoofed colleague or friend.

🧐

Curiosity

"Is this you in this photo?" or "Salary list attached."

🎭

Pretexting

An invented story, such as a fake survey or HR audit, to justify asking for information.

🪤

Baiting

Leaving infected USB drives or offering free downloads that hide malware.

Rule of thumb: if a message makes you feel rushed, scared, or excited, pause. That emotion is often the attack.

6. Best practices to avoid falling victim

  • Pause before you click. Hover over links and check the real destination.
  • Verify through another channel. Call the organization using a number from its official website, not from the message.
  • Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA) on every important account, so a stolen password alone is not enough.
  • Use a password manager and unique passwords. A manager will not autofill on a fake domain, which is a helpful warning.
  • Never share passwords, OTP codes, or PINs with anyone, including "support staff".
  • Keep software updated: operating system, browser, and antivirus.
  • Be careful with attachments, especially .zip, .exe, and Office files asking you to "enable macros".
  • Limit what you share publicly. Attackers use social media details to craft convincing spear phishing.
  • Report suspicious messages to your IT or security team, then delete them. Reporting protects your colleagues too.
  • If you clicked or entered data: change the password immediately, enable MFA, disconnect the device if you opened a file, and tell your security team right away.

7. Real-world examples

💼 The $100M+ fake-invoice scam (Google & Facebook, 2013–2015)

A fraudster posed as Quanta Computer, a real hardware supplier used by both companies, and sent convincing invoices and emails. The finance teams paid over $100 million before the fraud was detected. Most of the money was later recovered, and the attacker was prosecuted.

Lesson: even tech giants fall for Business Email Compromise. Verify payment changes and new bank details through a second channel.

🏛️ The 2016 Democratic National Committee email compromise

Staff received emails that looked like Google security alerts asking them to reset their passwords. Entering credentials on the fake page gave attackers access to mailboxes, and the stolen emails were later leaked.

Lesson: "security alert" emails are a favorite disguise. Go to the real site directly, and use MFA.

📦 Parcel-delivery smishing (ongoing worldwide)

People get texts saying a package could not be delivered and asking for a small "redelivery fee" on a look-alike page, which then steals card details. These campaigns pose as many different courier companies.

Lesson: couriers rarely ask for payment by text link. Use the official app or website to track parcels.

🔐 Fake "password expires today" IT notices (common in workplaces)

Employees receive an email pretending to be from IT saying their password expires today, with a link to a fake single sign-on page that captures their login.

Lesson: your real IT team will not ask you to log in through an emailed link. Confirm with them directly.

9. Summary

1️⃣

Stop

Do not react to pressure or emotion.

2️⃣

Inspect

Sender, links, tone, attachments, and the real domain.

3️⃣

Verify

Confirm through a separate, trusted channel.

Protect
